The problem behind the architecture

Ontology engineering turns a dataset into an explicit model of its meaning: classes, properties and relationships that other systems can use. It combines interpretation, formal modelling and data transformation.

During development, asking agents to perform several complex steps led to hallucinations and errors. I designed specialised roles to narrow each task and the context it needed.

My contribution: I developed the concept, designed and implemented the system, and wrote the manuscript. Other members of the research team carried out the validation.

The system, from input to graph

The controller assembles inputs from application state and previous outputs. The shared LLM client sends a system role and a task-specific message; it does not automatically append the full conversation history to those text calls.

That makes the information crossing each boundary explicit. Keeping it complete and up to date becomes an engineering responsibility.

Two decisions that shaped the system

Decision 01

Separate interpretation from formalisation

PlanSage works on the intended model; OntoBuilder expresses it in Turtle; OntoMapper connects it to source data. This addressed the errors I observed when too many complex steps were combined.

Trade-off: smaller tasks create more boundaries. Names, relationships and constraints must survive the handoff. Specialisation does not, by itself, establish semantic correctness.

Decision 02

Check generated artifacts with executable tools

The ontology path attempts RDF parsing and sends syntax feedback, including the problematic output, into a bounded retry loop. The generation entry point allows up to four attempts.

Trade-off: a valid Turtle document can still model the domain incorrectly. Syntax checking supports the workflow; expert evaluation answers a different question.

Results, with their conditions

The team compared generated and human-developed ontologies across six commercial datasets. The assisted workflow saved time in every case, while the qualitative assessment generally favoured human modelling. Generated ontologies were richer in annotations but retained modelling errors.

The study used expert review, OQuaRE and OOPS! Its results concern the complete assisted workflow; they do not isolate the effect of agent specialisation. I did not conduct this validation.

What I would improve today

I would preserve the data profile, schema and external alignments as separate, versioned artifacts. In the inspected code, the interoperability call replaces PlanSage’s answer, which OntoBuilder then consumes. That creates a risk of losing part of the original schema.

I would also replace fragile text delimiters with validated structured outputs. For mappings, I would make the retry policy explicit: the inspected controller accepts feedback, but its automatic retry loop is commented out.

These are present-day observations from source inspection, not newly reproduced runtime failures.
